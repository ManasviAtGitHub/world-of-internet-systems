World of Internet Systems · The Flagships · Episode 07

The Message

The most extreme efficiency story on the internet: about fifty engineers ran a message switch for nearly a billion people, by betting everything on Erlang, FreeBSD, bare metal, and ruthless minimalism. WhatsApp's server keeps no history, reads no content, and does exactly one thing — hold your message until the other phone shows up. This episode proves why that one thing is the whole design.

Every dot is one message. A lock glyph means ciphertext — content the server carries but cannot read. Phones flicker offline the way real mobile networks do; that flakiness is the enemy every experiment below is fighting.

Store-and-forward: the design that survives mobile reality

A phone on a mobile network is not "online" the way a server is. It rides elevators, enters tunnels, dies at 2%, switches towers. Any design that requires both phones to be reachable at the same moment is fighting physics. WhatsApp's answer is the oldest one in telecom: the server is a switch with a mailbox, not a pipe. Senders hand the message to the switch and are done; the switch holds it until the recipient's socket reappears, then deletes it.

Below, the same message traffic flows through both worlds while phones flicker realistically. Drag the flakiness up and watch which world degrades.

Two million phones on one computer

The switch only works if you can afford to keep an open TCP socket to every phone on Earth — that's the expensive part. In the old thread-per-connection server model, each connection costs a thread stack, megabytes of memory: the box drowns around ten thousand connections. That's the famous C10K wall. WhatsApp's Erlang runtime instead gives each connection a featherweight process — kilobytes — scheduled across all cores. In 2011 they held 1 million TCP connections on one FreeBSD box; in 2012, 2 million (2.8M in benchmarks) on a dual-hex-core Xeon with 100 GB of RAM. Then they deliberately backed off to ~1M per box, keeping headroom for the day a neighboring server dies and its million phones stampede over.

Group chats: encrypt once, not N times

End-to-end encryption has an awkward corollary: if only endpoints hold keys, then a message to a group of 128 naively means the sender's phone encrypts and uploads 128 separate copies — over a mobile uplink, on a battery. The Signal protocol's answer, Sender Keys, restores the server-side fan-out without giving the server the keys: the sender distributes a group key once through pairwise channels, then every message is encrypted once; the server copies the sealed envelope to each member, able to route all of them and read none of them.

The numbers that made it a legend

At the 2014 acquisition: ~450 million users on ~550 servers with 32 engineers. Later that year: 465M monthly users, 147M concurrent connections, 342K messages/sec inbound and 712K/sec outbound at peak, and an in-RAM Mnesia database holding 18 billion records in about 2 TB of memory. By 2015, Wired's famous framing: fifty engineers for 900 million users. Two billion users by 2020, roughly 100 billion messages a day per Meta's earnings remarks — all on the same skeleton: sealed envelopes, pigeonholes, and sockets held open by a runtime built for telephone switches.

The pattern to keep: do less, at greater depth. No feed, no history, no server-side cleverness — one flow, engineered to its physical limits. Every experiment above is a consequence of refusing a feature.
