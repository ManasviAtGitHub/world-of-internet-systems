The Heap

The structure that answers "what matters most right now?" without ever sorting anything. It's been working for you this whole series: merging LinkedIn's twelve partition lists into one feed, picking Pinterest's top pins out of a firehose, deciding which timer fires next inside the Go runtime. A heap makes exactly one promise — the root wins — and that single, deliberately lazy promise is why it's everywhere. Predict first, then run it. That's the rep.

Everything below computes for real: the hero is a genuine binary min-heap whose invariant is re-verified sixty times a second, the comparison race really sorts a million numbers against a real streaming heap and counts every comparison, and both schedulers eat the same seeded job stream.

The array trick

The drawing above shows a tree; the memory underneath is a flat array. Node i's children live at 2i+1 and 2i+2, its parent at (i−1)>>1 — the shape is arithmetic, so there are no pointers to chase, no allocations per node, and the whole structure walks cache lines instead of the wilds of RAM. This is why heaps back real production hot paths: the Go runtime keeps every pending timer in one, and your OS has one at the core of its event loops.

The second thing worth re-learning: building a heap from n existing items is O(n), not O(n log n). Sift-down work is proportional to a node's height, and almost all nodes are leaves with height zero — the sum telescopes. Interviewers love this because the "obvious" answer is wrong by a log factor.

Now the prediction. A million random numbers stream past and you want the top 100. The blunt way is to sort everything. The heap way keeps a min-heap of just 100: each arriving item is compared against the root — the weakest member of the current elite — and only replaces it if it's bigger. Sorting a million items costs about twenty million comparisons. What does the heap way cost? Commit before you run. The textbook answer, n·log k, is one of the four buttons — and it is not what the meter will say.

Priority is a policy, and order is the whole policy

A queue answers "who's next?" with whoever arrived first. A heap lets you answer with anything you can compare — and in production the comparison that matters most is the deadline. The Workspace episode watched a job queue wedge when a flood of bulk work parked in front of urgent work; this is that story, isolated. The keeper

The keeper: a heap is the cheapest possible answer to "who's next?" — O(1) to ask, log n to maintain, and totally unsorted everywhere the question doesn't reach. When you see top-K, merge-K, soonest-deadline, or nearest-anything in an interview, the heap is the shape of the answer; when you see scatter-gather feeds, timer wheels, or schedulers in production, it was already there.

The revision card — for the you that comes back in three months
  • Why is a heap deliberately not sorted? (sorting costs log n per op everywhere; the heap pays it only at the root)
  • Top-k of n: where does the work actually go? (one root check per item; only ~k·ln(n/k) items ever sift — you watched ~1M, not 7M)
  • Merge k sorted lists: what does the bill scale with? (what you take × log k — the sort never noticed you wanted only 40)
  • What does a priority heap not give you? (capacity — it only chooses who absorbs the wait, and bulk paid it)
