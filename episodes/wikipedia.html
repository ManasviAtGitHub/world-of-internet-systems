World of Internet Systems · Episode 30 · The Finale

The Encyclopedia

The last system in this series is the one that shouldn't be possible: a top-10 website — 296 billion pageviews in 2024, a billion devices a month — running on about 1,300 servers and a hosting bill of $3.4 million, operated by a nonprofit whose runbooks are a public wiki. Wikipedia works because its workload is merciful in one dimension (about 600 reads for every write) and brutal in another: any page can change at any second, and when vandalism is fixed it must vanish now, from every cache on Earth. This finale runs the funnel, the purge, the two cache physics, and the million-page blast radius of editing one template.

Everything below is honestly simulated: the funnel's survival counts multiply through published hit rates, both purge worlds serve the same vandalism timeline, the cache-physics numbers are load and capacity arithmetic, and the re-parse storm drains at a real fleet's real rate.

Purge, don't expire

Most CDNs accept staleness: set a TTL, let the edge serve old copies until the clock runs out. Wikipedia can't. When someone defaces a page and a volunteer fixes it thirty seconds later, "the fix will propagate within 24 hours" is not an answer. So every edit sends explicit purge messages to every cache node on every continent — the old HTML is hunted down and deleted worldwide within seconds — with conservative TTLs kept only as a backstop against lost purges. The experiment below runs the same incident in both worlds, and it's honest about the symmetry: purging also makes the vandalism visible faster. Freshness cuts both ways; what you're buying is control after the fix.

Two cache physics

Inside each site, the edge is two tiers with opposite philosophies. Varnish, in RAM at the front, holds the hits — and holds them on every node, because when an article goes viral you want all eight machines serving it at memory speed, not one unlucky owner melting (episode 17's hot partition, wearing a cache). Apache Traffic Server, on disk behind, holds the long tail — hash-partitioned so each URL lives on exactly one node, because fifty million obscure articles need capacity, and replicating them eight times would divide it by eight. Replication for popularity, partitioning for capacity: the experiment lets you turn off either tier and watch which failure you bought.

One template, two million pages

A wiki page isn't a file — it's a program. Wikitext pulls in templates, which pull in other templates and Lua modules, and rendering it all to HTML is the most expensive step in the stack (which is why ParserCache keeps the result for weeks). The nightmare scenario is written into that design: edit one popular infobox template and two million pages are suddenly wrong. Re-parsing them all immediately would burn hours of the entire parser fleet while every ordinary page render queues behind the storm. MediaWiki's answer: invalidation is instant and free — mark everything dirty — but re-rendering is lazy. Pages someone actually views get re-parsed on demand (that reader pays one parse, once); the job queue re-parses the cold tail in the background at whatever rate the fleet can spare. The edit's cost is real, but it's paid where and when it matters.

The budget with a log axis

Now the punchline that makes Wikipedia the right finale. The Wikimedia Foundation's entire annual budget is about $189 million; the actual internet-hosting line inside it is roughly $3.4 million. Alphabet's capital expenditure in 2024 — one year of building — was about $52.5 billion: four orders of magnitude, for the same planet of users. The gap isn't magic and it isn't martyrdom; it's the architecture on this page. When 600 reads share every write, when 90% of requests die at the edge, when the parse is cached for weeks and invalidation is an event instead of a timer, the marginal pageview costs approximately nothing — and an encyclopedia can be run on donations. Frugality wasn't the constraint on the design; it was the design. And uniquely on the entire internet, you can check all of it: the CDN docs, the incident postmortems, the datacenter-switchover runbook (drilled routinely, reads never stopping) are a public wiki.

The series, closed

Thirty systems, and the same handful of truths kept surfacing wearing different uniforms. The queue that grows without bound whenever arrivals outrun service — we met it in episode 1's single server and again today in a parser fleet. The wall that turns a catastrophe into a bad afternoon: pods, partitions, bulkheads, geoshards. Precompute for the read-heavy: timelines, sort orders, feeds, and now an encyclopedia's HTML. Exactly-once as memory, not hope: idempotency keys, pair-keyed streams, atomic swaps. The boring-technology dividend, paid to Pinterest and Shopify and Wikipedia alike. And under all of it, this series' own method: a design you cannot simulate honestly is a design you do not yet understand. Every collapse on these pages collapsed by arithmetic; every fix fixed the arithmetic. That was the point.

The keeper, for the finale and the series: the internet's greatest systems are not the ones with the most machines — they are the ones that arranged for almost all the work to never need doing.
