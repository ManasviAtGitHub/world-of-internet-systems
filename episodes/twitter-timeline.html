The Timeline

In 2013 Twitter published its arithmetic: about 5,700 tweets written per second, and 300,000 home timelines read per second — call it sixty reads for every write. Every feed you've ever scrolled — Twitter, Instagram, LinkedIn — is built on one decision about that asymmetry: do you assemble the timeline when someone opens it, or when someone posts to it? Both answers are wrong. The interesting part is why, and what survives.

Amber = writes (tweets, fan-out inserts). Cyan = reads (timeline fetches). The datastores below are honest fluid queues: work arrives, capacity drains it, backlog is the difference. Capacities are scaled down ~1,000× so you can watch.

Pull or push: the only two ways to build a feed

Pull (fan-out-on-read) is what you'd write first: store each user's tweets once; when a reader opens the app, fetch recent tweets from everyone they follow and merge. Writes are cheap — one insert. Reads cost one fetch per followed account.

Push (fan-out-on-write) inverts it: when a tweet is posted, insert its ID into a precomputed timeline for every follower — Twitter kept these as Redis lists, capped at roughly 800 entries per user. Reads become one cheap fetch (1ms median, 4ms p99 in 2013); writes are amplified by follower count.

Same total work, shuffled between the two sides of the ledger. The experiment below runs both against the same traffic. Drag the read rate up to Twitter-like asymmetry and watch which ledger goes bankrupt.

The monster inside push

Push won at Twitter, and the machinery was lovely: a fanout service walked each new tweet's follower list in batches of about 4,000, inserting the tweet ID into follower timelines, targeting delivery to every follower within about five seconds (Krikorian, "Timelines at Scale", 2013).

Then Lady Gaga arrives with 31 million followers. One tap of hers is 31 million inserts — minutes of work for the fanout fleet, in real life, at real Twitter. And because the fanout queue is shared, everyone behind her waits: your friend's lunch tweet sits in line while the machine grinds through a celebrity's fan-out. Below is that machine. Press the button. Then flip the switch that saved the design: don't fan out celebrities at all — readers merge celebrity tweets in at read time, a pull-shaped island inside a push system.

So where exactly is the line?

Hybrid needs a rule for who counts as a celebrity — a follower threshold. Here is the genuine surprise: you cannot derive that threshold from operation counts. Follow the ledger for one follow edge. Push charges the author's tweet rate — one insert per tweet. Pull charges the follower's read rate — one merge check per timeline load. Follower count multiplies both sides equally, then cancels out of the comparison. Efficiency arithmetic alone never draws a celebrity line. (We know because we tried: the first version of this panel was a cost-minimum curve, and the math flatly refused to produce one.)

What actually draws the line is a promise: every follower sees your tweet within about five seconds. A fanout job is one indivisible lump — followers × one insert — and while it grinds, everything behind it waits, as EXP-02 just showed. So the real rule is simple: no single tweet may occupy the pipe for longer than the delivery promise. That turns the celebrity line into arithmetic you can draw.

What the timeline taught everyone else

The 2013 architecture carried Twitter through its record 143,199 tweets-per-second moment without a visible blip. Its ideas became the standard kit: Instagram, LinkedIn, and effectively every feed since runs some flavor of push-with-exceptions. The mirror image is worth keeping too: Twitter's own search index, Earlybird, made the opposite bet — index once (cheap write), scatter-gather across all partitions per query (expensive read) — because search queries were rare compared to tweets. Same ledger, opposite asymmetry, opposite answer. The design rule underneath both: amplify on the rare side of your traffic, never the abundant one.

Since 2016 the timeline's contents have been chosen by ranking rather than pure recency — a candidate funnel Twitter open-sourced in 2023: roughly 1,500 candidate tweets per refresh, half from your network and half discovered, pruned by a light model, scored by a ~48-million-parameter heavy ranker. But that funnel sits on top of the delivery machinery you just watched; fan-out still decides what's in the pool. Plumbing first, intelligence second.
