The Log

In 2010, LinkedIn needed to move activity data between systems, so it rebuilt the humblest structure in computing — the append-only log — into a service. That service, Kafka, now carries seven trillion messages a day at LinkedIn alone and quietly underpins most of the internet's data plumbing. This episode proves the two things a log buys you: failure becomes delay, and nothing is ever lost to a crash.

Amber = messages and the log itself. Cyan = the directly-coupled world we compare against. The queues below are honest fluid simulations — arrival rates, capacities, backlog — scaled down so one dot ≈ many thousands of real messages.

What a queue is actually for

Call one service directly from another and you have signed two contracts you didn't read. First: you can only ever go as fast as your slowest consumer. Second: their crash is now your outage. Both contracts come due at the worst moment — during the traffic spike, during the deploy.

A log in the middle rewrites them. The producer appends and moves on, never waiting. The consumer pulls at its own pace from its own offset. When traffic spikes past the consumer's capacity, nothing breaks — the consumer simply falls behind, and the gap has a name, lag, and a burndown chart. The crash, and the rewind button

Now the second contract. Kill the consumer outright — a deploy gone wrong, an OOM, a full disk. In the direct world, whatever the producer sends during the outage is refused or fired into the void; when the consumer returns, that history is simply gone. In the log world the consumer's offset just… stops. The log keeps appending. The consumer comes back, reads from where it left off, and catches up. Messages are not deleted when read — Kafka retains them for days by policy — which also buys you the operation no direct pipeline can offer: rewind. Reset your offset into the past and reprocess history — the fix for "our code had a bug for the last half hour."

The quieter revolution: N×M becomes N+M

Jay Kreps' 2013 essay "The Log" opens with an uglier problem than spikes: integration sprawl. N systems produce data, M systems want it, and point-to-point pipelines multiply toward N×M bespoke connections — each with its own format, failure modes, and lag. Put one durable, ordered log in the middle and the count collapses to N+M: everyone writes to the log, everyone reads from the log, at their own pace, none aware the others exist.

How fast can a dumb log go?

Absurdly. Because the broker keeps no per-message state and writes sequentially, it rides the disk and the OS page cache at full speed: Kreps' 2014 benchmark pushed 2 million writes per second on three cheap machines. The growth curve at Kafka's birthplace tells the rest: 1.1 trillion messages/day at LinkedIn in 2015, 1.4 trillion in 2016, 7 trillion a day by 2019 across 100+ clusters and seven million partitions. Netflix's Keystone pipeline moved ~700 billion events a day back in 2016; Uber measures its Kafka traffic in trillions of messages and petabytes daily.

Durability, when you want it, is a dial rather than a prayer: each partition keeps a leader and replicas, an in-sync replica set tracks who's caught up, and acks chooses your point on the fast-and-lossy ↔ slow-and-safe axis — Netflix famously ran early pipelines at the lossy end on purpose, then tightened after loss incidents. And the smart-consumer design has one more gift: within one consumer group, partitions divide across members for parallelism; across groups, everyone gets everything — queue semantics and broadcast semantics from the same bytes.
