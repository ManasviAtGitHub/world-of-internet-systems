World of Internet Systems · Internet Plumbing

The Name

Before any page loads, a quieter machine must turn the name you typed into a number routers can use. DNS does this with a hierarchy so nobody has to know everything, and caching so aggressive that almost no query ever travels the full path. Thirteen root addresses — answered by more than two thousand servers — sit at the top, and when the system fails, the internet doesn't slow down. It vanishes.

Time is slowed ~30×: a full uncached lookup takes 20–120 ms in real life and a few watchable seconds here. TTLs are compressed the same way. Green pulse = successful resolution, red = failure or a stale answer.

The real DNS is the cache

The full walk — root, TLD, authoritative — is the rare path. Every answer carries a TTL: "you may believe this for N seconds," the internet's most successful eventual-consistency contract. Resolvers hold answers for that long, so the overwhelming majority of the world's DNS queries die in a cache within a few milliseconds and the upstream hierarchy never hears about them.

The experiment below is the counterfactual the designers of 1987 got right in advance. Forty lookups a second flow against fifty domains. Turn caching off and every single one walks the full tree — watch the root and TLD meters melt. Turn it on and the hierarchy drops to a trickle, because a cache absorbs exactly the repetition that popularity creates.

The TTL contract cuts both ways

So make every TTL a week and go home? No — because the same contract that grants resilience also forbids fast change. Move your site to a new address and every resolver on Earth is entitled to keep serving the old one until its copy of your promise expires. Below, a site fails over to a new IP. The stale answers don't stop when you flip the switch — they stop when the TTL says so. Shorten the TTL and recovery is quick, but your authoritative server answers queries all day. This dial has no free setting.

October 21, 2016 — the day the names went out

Now aim the failure at the other side of the system. Dyn was the authoritative provider for a huge slice of famous sites. That morning, the Mirai botnet — tens of millions of hijacked cameras and DVRs conscripted through roughly sixty default passwords — fired attack waves reported near 1.2 Tbps at Dyn's servers. Twitter, Spotify, GitHub, Netflix, PayPal "went down" for hours. Their servers were fine. Nobody could resolve their names.

Except — not everyone, and not all at once. Every resolver with a still-fresh cached answer kept working, oblivious. The outage rolled across the internet in TTL order: each site survived exactly as long as the caches' remaining promises. Run it yourself.

What to keep

Thirteen addresses is a costume, not a capacity. Each root identity is anycast — announced from hundreds of places at once, so "asking F-root" means asking whichever of its 366 instances is nearest. As of 2026 the thirteen addresses resolve to 2,004 servers run by twelve independent operators; the roots have shrugged off multi-million-queries-per-second attacks that users never noticed.

DNS is a dependency amplifier. It sits before everything, so its failure impersonates the failure of everything behind it — and failing clients retry in a loop. When Facebook's routes vanished in 2021, resolvers that had nothing to do with Facebook ate a 30× query storm from retrying apps. Design accordingly: the phone book gets called hardest exactly when it has nothing to say.
