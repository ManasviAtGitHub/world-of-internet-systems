The Drop

Shopify's defining workload is a stampede with a credit card: a celebrity drop aims the internet at one shop, and Black Friday aims it at all of them — $5.1 million in sales per minute at the 2025 peak, 284 million requests per minute at the edge. What's underneath is the internet's loudest argument for boring architecture: one Ruby on Rails monolith, sliced into isolated pods of shops so that a fire stays in its cell, with exactly one piece — the read path — carved out and rebuilt for speed. This episode burns a pod, splits the paths, and prices the microservices tax.

Everything below is honestly simulated: the shared-database collapse and the pod firewall come from the same queue arithmetic with the same total hardware, the storefront p75 emerges from a real cache-hit distribution, and the monolith-vs-microservices numbers are per-hop latency and failure probability, compounded — not adjectives.

One shop on fire, eleven sleeping soundly

Here is multi-tenancy's nightmare arithmetic. Put every shop in one big shared database — even a very big one — and a single celebrity drop can demand more writes than the whole cluster serves. Queues don't care whose writes they hold: the moment the shared queue forms, every shop's checkout slows, and a sneaker drop in Los Angeles breaks a florist in Ghent. Shopify's answer (2015, after vertical scaling hit its ceiling) was to make the shard boundary a failure boundary: pods own their datastores outright, so the same hardware, cut into twelve cells, turns a platform outage into one shop's very bad — and survivable — afternoon. The hot shop's buyers wait in a throttled checkout line; orders complete at exactly the pod's capacity; nobody else notices.

Carve out the reads, keep the monolith

During a drop, page views outnumber checkouts a hundred to one — and for fifteen years both ran through the same Rails monolith. Every browse burned a worker that a checkout needed. Shopify's fix (2019–2020) was surgical, not doctrinal: extract exactly one thing — storefront rendering, the read-only path — into a purpose-built app, the Storefront Renderer, with layered caching: hottest data in-process, frequent data in a key-value store, the rest computed. It shipped by shadow-verifying its responses against the monolith's for months, then took over with p75 under ~45ms — four times faster — while checkout kept the monolith to itself.

The tax the network charges

The obvious question: why isn't Shopify a thousand microservices? Their 2019 answer — "Deconstructing the Monolith" — became the canonical defense of staying put: they reorganized ~6,000 classes into business-domain components and built Packwerk, tooling that fails CI when one component reaches into another's internals. Modularity, without distribution. Because distribution has a price you pay on every request: an in-process call costs microseconds and fails approximately never; a network hop costs milliseconds and fails at some rate that compounds across every hop in the chain. The experiment below charges that tax honestly — and the note admits what the monolith gives up in return.

Rehearsing the flood

The part with no simulation: BFCM readiness is a nine-month engineering season. Capacity models start in March; a "What Could Go Wrong" census feeds game days where engineers inject real faults into production-like systems; the internal load generator, Genghis, fires five-plus full-platform scale tests a year — 200 million requests per minute across three cloud regions in 2025. The weekend itself reads like a launch: 10.5 trillion database queries, 1.17 trillion writes, a MySQL fleet peaking past 19 million queries per second, five-nines uptime. The flood is scheduled; so is the practice.

The keeper: Shopify scaled by choosing its cuts — shops into pods for isolation, reads from writes for speed, components behind walls instead of services behind wires — and rehearsing the one day a year the internet aims at it.
