The Onsale

Selling seats is the purest concurrency problem on the internet: every seat is unique, demand outstrips supply ten to a hundred times over, the entire sale happens in minutes on purpose — and every double-sell is a lawsuit. On November 15, 2022, the Eras Tour onsale put the numbers on record: 3.5 billion requests in a day, four times Ticketmaster's previous peak, against roughly two million sellable seats. This episode runs the machinery that makes an intentional thundering herd survivable: the hold with a countdown, the lottery that beats the bots, and the wall of caches protecting a 1970s engine.

Honesty note: Ticketmaster publishes far less engineering detail than this series' other subjects. The waiting room, random queue assignment, throttled admission, ~10-minute holds, and the VAX-era core are disclosed; the lock and cache mechanics simulated here are standard reservation-system patterns, labeled as such — every collapse and recovery below is still real queue arithmetic.

The hold with a countdown

A seat is not a quantity — it's a specific chair, and a human needs minutes to decide while holding it. The reservation pattern [standard, with the ~10-minute hold disclosed]: choosing seats grants an exclusive, expiring claim — AVAILABLE → HELD(expires) → SOLD, with a sweeper returning abandoned holds to the pool. The alternative — optimistic checkout, verify at commit — means two fans can both fill in card details for seat 14F and one finds out after typing their CVV. Ticketing chooses pessimistic holds because the item is unique and the human is slow. But the TTL is a genuine dial with pain on both ends: too short and holds expire mid-payment; too long and abandoned carts strangle the supply.

The lottery at the gate

Now the herd itself. A first-come sale is a latency race, and latency races have a permanent champion: the bot, reacting in 30 milliseconds from a datacenter next door, versus a human reading a screen in two seconds. Open the gates at 10:00:00 and speed decides everything — plus millions of simultaneous arrivals crush the inventory tier. The disclosed design breaks both problems at once: everyone in the waiting room at sale time gets a random position (arriving 20 minutes early buys nothing), and the resulting FIFO queue admits a few hundred shoppers at a time. Randomization converts the race bots always win into a lottery weighted only by headcount; throttling means the fragile tier sees bounded concurrency no matter how many millions wait.

Bedrock: protect the engine that cannot scale

At the bottom of all of it sits a fact almost too good to be true: the system of record for seat inventory is a lineal descendant of Ticketmaster's original ticketing engine — written largely in VAX assembly on a custom OS in the late 1970s, running today under software emulation, wrapped by Java APIs and deployed around by Kubernetes. It is fast, it is correct, and it is one machine's worth of throughput, forever. The architecture around it exists to spend that scarce resource only on what matters: writes. Seat-map reads — millions of fans refreshing — are served from cached snapshots that regenerate every couple of seconds. The cost is honesty on the map: the seat you're looking at may have died seconds ago, which is exactly why your click sometimes bounces politely and the purchase never lies. [Core: disclosed. Cache mechanics: standard pattern.]

What the onsale teaches

Ticketing's lesson generalizes to every drop, launch, and registration day on the internet — which is why the waiting room became a product category (Queue-it, Cloudflare Waiting Room) and the reservation-with-TTL pattern shows up in airline seat maps and hotel checkouts. Demand you cannot serve, you must shape: move the fight earlier (registration and scoring), replace races with lotteries (bots lose their only advantage), meter the herd into bounded concurrency, and let an expiring claim — not a payment failure — be the way a human loses a seat.

The keeper: when supply is fixed and demand arrives as a vertical wall, the system's job is not to be fast — it's to be fair, bounded, and incapable of selling the same chair twice, in that order.
