World of Internet Systems · Internet Plumbing · Episode 08

The Border

The internet is not one network. It's about 78,000 independently-run networks — ISPs, clouds, universities, Facebook — that agree on how to reach a million address blocks by shouting routes at each other and mostly believing what they hear. That protocol is BGP, and its trust model is how one bad command deleted Facebook for six hours, and how one Pakistani ISP accidentally became YouTube for the entire planet.

Every box below is an autonomous system running real path-vector routing: candidate routes per neighbor, shortest-path selection, longest-prefix forwarding. Amber dots are packets; cyan pulses are route announcements; red means dropped or hijacked. ~78,000 networks are compressed to twelve.

The self-healing rumor mill

Because every AS keeps candidate routes from each neighbor and continuously re-selects the best (business policy first, then shortest AS-path), the map heals itself. Cut a cable and withdrawals ripple out; every affected router falls back to its next-best candidate and re-announces. Nobody coordinates. The internet reroutes around damage the way rumor networks survive any one gossip going quiet.

February 24, 2008: Pakistan becomes YouTube

Pakistan Telecom was ordered to block YouTube domestically. Their tool of choice: announce 208.65.153.0/24 — a more-specific slice of YouTube's 208.65.152.0/22 — into a blackhole. Their upstream propagated it to the world, and BGP's iron rule did the rest: longest prefix match beats everything. A /24 wins over a covering /22 no matter whose path is shorter or whose servers are real. Within minutes, the planet's YouTube traffic poured into Islamabad and vanished. YouTube's counter-move used the very same rule: announce two /25s — sharper than the /24 — and pull the traffic back. The whole duel took about two and a quarter hours, minute-by-minute in RIPE's route collectors.

October 4, 2021: Facebook erases itself

No attacker this time. A routine maintenance command — meant to measure backbone capacity — disconnected Facebook's entire backbone instead (the audit tool that should have stopped it had a bug). Then a safety mechanism finished the job: Facebook's DNS servers are designed to withdraw their own BGP routes when they can't reach the data centers, so they don't serve stale answers. With the backbone down, all of them concluded they were unhealthy — and withdrew. At 15:58 UTC the prefixes were gone from the global table. The data centers were healthy. The servers were running. And no route on Earth led to them.

What to keep

The internet is a federation, not a network. BGP's real product is sovereignty: every AS applies its own private commercial policy and reveals nothing about why. That's what let the internet grow without a ruler — and why nobody can simply fix it from the center. Its two catastrophic properties — believe-anything trust and longest-prefix supremacy — are inseparable from its two miraculous ones: no coordinator, and automatic healing. RPKI is the thirty-years-late deed registry being bolted on: signed proof of "who may originate this block," now covering about two-thirds of announced routes — origin validation only, moving at the speed of the slowest incentive.
