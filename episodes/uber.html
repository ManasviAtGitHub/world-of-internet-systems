The Match

Uber turned "where is everyone right now?" into a data-structure problem. Every driver's phone reports its position about every four seconds — a design target of a million location writes per second by 2015 — and the marketplace answers with a match in moments, roughly 33 million times a day. The machinery: index the planet as hexagons, batch the matching, and let the price itself be a feedback controller, computed cell by cell on the same grid.

Amber dots are drivers; cyan dots are riders waiting. The hexagons are the real idea — Uber's open-source H3 grid — and every experiment below runs on genuinely simulated movement and matching, scaled to one small city.

Why hexagons, and why an index at all

H3 projects the Earth onto an icosahedron and tiles it with hexagons at sixteen resolutions — each cell splitting into ~7 children, 122 base cells, and the twelve unavoidable pentagons parked in oceans. Hexagons because they have exactly one neighbor distance (squares have two, triangles three), which makes "look at my neighborhood" a clean, uniform operation for everything from dispatch to pricing.

But the deeper point is what any spatial index buys. "Find drivers near this rider" has a naive implementation: check every driver in the city. The index implementation checks one ring of cells. The experiment below runs both against the same city and counts the work.

The two-second pause that shortens every ride

Greedy dispatch — assign each rider instantly to the nearest idle driver — feels optimal and isn't. The rider who requests first grabs a driver that the rider two blocks away needed far more, and the city pays for the myopia in extra driving. Uber's DISCO treats dispatch as a continuously re-solved optimization — Matt Ranney described it as a rolling traveling-salesman problem across the city. The simplest version of that idea: wait a beat, then match the pool. Both cities below receive identical riders; one matches instantly, the other holds a two-second batch and assigns the pool globally.

Surge: the thermostat on the grid

When a concert lets out, one neighborhood suddenly holds a hundred riders and four idle drivers. No matching cleverness fixes a supply deficit. Uber's answer is computed on the same hexagons: the demand/supply ratio per cell sets a price multiplier, which does two things at once — some riders wait or walk (demand tempered), and idle drivers across town see the hot cells and drift toward them (supply summoned). Whether surge is fair is a policy debate; what the simulation can show is what it does to the queue.

What holds it up underneath

The dispatch engine itself is sharded with the same trick this series keeps meeting: a consistent-hash ring. Uber's Ringpop nodes gossip membership (SWIM) and divide the city's work; any node can take any request and forward it to the owner. Trip state outlived a 2014 Postgres crisis via Schemaless — an append-only sharded-MySQL store later evolved into Docstore (Raft-replicated partitions, tens of millions of QPS) — and the 2021 fulfillment rewrite moved live trips onto Spanner. The wildest resilience trick: for datacenter failover, dispatch periodically ships an encrypted state digest to driver phones; if a datacenter dies, the fleet of phones uploads the digests to the survivor and reconstructs every in-progress trip. A million phones as the backup datastore.

The keeper pattern: put everything on one grid. Supply, demand, ETAs, and price all speak hexagon — so "nearby," "busy," and "expensive" are the same cheap lookup, and the marketplace can think about the city the way the city actually works: locally.
