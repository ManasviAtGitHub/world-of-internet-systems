The Ladder

Every hypergrowth company climbs the same ladder — one box, then a cache, then read replicas, then partitions, then shards — and every rung quietly trades away another piece of the comforting single-machine illusion. Figma bought four years of runway before paying the sharding toll; Instagram paid it with two people. This episode climbs the ladder live, then runs the three traps waiting on the way up: the vanishing comment, the write that evaporated, and the election that must never split.

Everything below is honestly simulated: replication lag is real delay, the durability race uses real crash timing, and EXP-03 is a genuine Raft election — terms, votes, randomized timeouts, and majority rule actually implemented.

Trap one: the vanishing comment

Read replicas are the easiest rung — copy the leader, send reads to the copies. But async replication means every replica is a time machine set slightly in the past. The classic casualty: a user posts a comment, the page refreshes, the read lands on a lagging replica — and their own comment is gone. It isn't lost; it just hasn't arrived where they're looking. The standard fix is sticky reads: after you write, your session reads from the leader until the replicas catch up.

Trap two: the write that evaporated

Async replication has a darker corner than staleness. The leader acknowledges your write, you see "saved" — and then the leader dies before the replica received it. When the follower is promoted, your acknowledged write never happened. Synchronous replication closes the hole: the leader doesn't ack until a follower confirms — no acknowledged write can die with one machine — and the bill arrives on every single write as added latency. MySQL and Postgres both ship async by default; the dial is yours.

Trap three: who's in charge? (a real Raft election)

Replicas and failover need an answer to the oldest distributed question: who is the leader right now — decided without a boss, and without ever splitting the brain. Raft's answer runs below, genuinely implemented: every node keeps a randomized election countdown that the leader's heartbeats keep resetting. Leader dies → first countdown to expire becomes a candidate for a new numbered term and asks for votes; each node votes once per term; a majority makes a leader. Majorities overlap, so two leaders can't both win the same term — and a partitioned minority can shout forever without electing anyone.

The rungs above, and what to believe

Past sharding, the ladder's upper rungs are stories this series has already run: the hash ring and logical shards (covered in the Hash Ring and the Feed), quorums and vector clocks (the Quorum), the log as replication itself (the Log). Two closing calibrations from the research. First, sharding strategy is a query decision: hash sharding spreads heat evenly but shatters range scans; range sharding keeps scans fast but lets time-ordered keys hammer the last shard — pick by what you ask, not what you store. Second: consistency claims are empirical claims. The Jepsen project partitions real databases and checks their promises; it has caught MongoDB losing committed writes at its strongest settings and PostgreSQL's serializable isolation admitting an anomaly — both later fixed, both proof that the only trustworthy consistency guarantee is a tested one.

This closes the plumbing tier — sixteen episodes from a single overloaded queue to the full ladder. The keeper for the tier's finale: every rung of scale sells a piece of the single-machine illusion; know exactly which piece you sold, because a trap on this page is waiting where it used to be.
