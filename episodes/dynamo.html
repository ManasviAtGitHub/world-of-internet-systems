The Quorum

Around 2004, Amazon made a business decision disguised as a technical one: the shopping cart must never refuse an item — not during a disk failure, not during a network partition, not on the biggest shopping day of the year. Accepting that requirement meant giving up strong consistency, and working out the consequences honestly produced the Dynamo paper — the document that launched the NoSQL era. This episode runs its machinery: real quorums, real staleness, real vector clocks.

N = 3 replicas throughout, like the paper. Amber = writes, cyan = reads, red = failures and stale data. Amazon states its targets at the 99.9th percentile — the slowest requests belong to the customers with the fullest carts.

The dial: R + W against N

Dynamo's consistency story is one inequality. Writes wait for W of the N replicas; reads consult R of them and take the newest version they see. If R + W > N, every read set must overlap every write set — somebody you ask has the newest value, guaranteed by counting. If R + W ≤ N, a read can land entirely on replicas the last write hasn't reached yet, and you serve the past. The experiment below measures it: live writes, live reads, live replication lag, and a stale counter that goes to exactly zero the moment the inequality holds.

Always writeable: the sloppy quorum

A strict quorum has a hidden availability cliff: if enough of the key's home replicas are down, writes fail — exactly what the cart is forbidden to do. Dynamo's answer: take the write anyway. The first N healthy nodes on the ring accept it, and each stand-in stores a hint — a luggage tag naming the true owner. When the home replica returns, the tagged writes walk home. Your add-to-cart succeeded during the outage; the price is a window of divergence, which the next experiment's machinery will clean up.

The split cart, and who may merge it

Eventual consistency has a bill, and it arrives as a conflict: during a partition, your phone adds a lamp to the cart while your laptop adds a book, on opposite sides of the split. Timestamps can't save you — "last write wins" just silently deletes someone's item. Dynamo's answer is vector clocks: each version carries per-node counters, so the system can tell "B descends from A" apart from "A and B are genuinely concurrent." Concurrent versions are both kept and handed to the application at read time — because only the application knows what merging means. For a cart: union. An add is never lost.

What became of it

The mandate that made this world possible predates the paper: around 2002, Bezos ordered that every team expose its work only through service interfaces — no shared databases, no back-doors, "anyone who doesn't do this will be fired" (as Steve Yegge memorably recounted). One amazon.com page render became a fan-out to 100–150 services; the order pipeline became queues and retries instead of one giant transaction; and the internal platforms that resulted were eventually sold to the world as AWS.

Dynamo's own afterlife is the best irony in the series: DynamoDB, the managed descendant launched in 2012, dropped the exotic parts — no sloppy quorum, no vector clocks — in favor of boring Paxos-replicated partitions, because operability beat theoretical elegance. The paper's real heirs are external: Cassandra, Riak, and Voldemort are all its children. And the scale kept compounding: DynamoDB peaked at 89 million requests per second on Prime Day 2021, 126M in 2023, 146M in 2024, and 151 million requests per second in 2025 — roughly every human on Earth pressing a button every 53 milliseconds — while Amazon's consumer business finished walking off Oracle entirely: 7,500 databases, 75 petabytes, gone by 2019.

The keeper: decide what must never fail, and spend consistency to buy it. Every distributed database since is an answer to where that dial should sit.
