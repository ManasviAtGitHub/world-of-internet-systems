The Eviction

Every cache is a bet about the future, and eviction is where the bet is placed. LRU — a hash map holding hands with a doubly-linked list — is the most-asked data structure design in interview history, and the policy running inside your CPU, your browser, and half the RAM of the internet. Its wager is one sentence: whatever was touched recently will be touched again. Under real traffic that wager pays astonishingly well, fails in one famous way, and the fix ships in memcached and InnoDB today. Predict first, then run it. That's the rep.

Everything below computes for real: the hero is an actual map-plus-linked-list LRU whose two structures are cross-verified every frame, every hit rate is measured against live streams, and the psychic oracle in the REP is a real cache that was simply told the future.

The interview card — where you've ground this before
  • "Design an LRU cache — O(1) get and put." (The most-asked structure design there is.)
  • "LRU vs LFU vs FIFO — when does each win?"
  • "Why do caches work at all? What hit rate can 10% of the catalog promise?"
Pattern
hash map + doubly-linked list · hit = move to front · evict = drop the tail
Space
O(capacity) — an entry plus two pointers each
Time
O(1) get, put, evict — the map finds, the list reorders
The catch
recency is a bet on the pattern — one cold scan flushes the whole working set, and the production fix is a probation segment

Why 10% of the shelf serves most of the store

Caching works because internet traffic is brutally concentrated — the Edge episode lived on that fact, and Zipf's law is its shape: the k-th most popular item gets ~1/k of the love. So the bet: a catalog of 10,000 items, Zipf traffic, and a cache that holds just 1,000 — one item in ten. What hit rate does plain LRU deliver? The uniform-world instinct says 10%. Commit; then three real caches run 400,000 accesses of one stream: your LRU, a cache that evicts at random, and a psychic oracle that was handed the popularity table in advance.

The scan that flushes everything

LRU's famous failure isn't exotic — it's Tuesday. A backup job, a crawler, an analytics query walks a few thousand cold keys once each. Plain LRU dutifully front-inserts every one of them, and your hot working set — the keys that earn the hit rate — drift off the tail. The fix production settled on is a bouncer's velvet rope: new keys enter a small probation segment, and only a second touch promotes them to the protected majority. One-time scanners churn probation and never touch the good stuff. memcached ships this as its segmented LRU; InnoDB does the same trick as "midpoint insertion" in its buffer pool. The policy tournament

FIFO, random, LRU, LFU — four bets about the future, one stream. Under stable popularity, frequency is the strongest signal there is: LFU effectively rebuilds the oracle's table. But real popularity drifts — yesterday's chart-topper fades — and pure LFU has no way to forget it (the same disease the Sketch cured with halving). Flip the drift switch and watch the ranking reshuffle. This is why Press Play's Open Connect fills by nightly popularity (LFU thinking, recomputed daily — frequency with a built-in clock), while general-purpose caches ship recency: LRU is never the best policy, and never the wrong one.

The keeper

The keeper: a cache is a bet, and LRU is the O(1) way to keep betting on recency — a map to find, a list to order, a tail to sacrifice. Under Zipf a 10% cache buys ~65% of requests, within points of psychic; one cold scan can flush it all, and a probation segment is the production-grade vaccine. When the interview says "design a cache," this page is the answer; when production says "the hit rate cratered at 2 a.m.," it was probably a scan.

The revision card — for the you that comes back in three months
  • Why is every LRU operation O(1)? (the map finds the node, the list re-links it — no scan anywhere; you watched the chain)
  • Why does a 10% cache catch ~65%? (Zipf concentration — and the psychic oracle only managed 75; recency rode for free)
  • What breaks LRU, and what's the shipping fix? (a one-pass cold scan; probation segments — memcached's segmented LRU, InnoDB's midpoint insertion)
  • When does LFU beat LRU, and when does it lose? (stable popularity vs drift — frequency is memory, recency is adaptation)
