The Board

Pinterest nearly died of interesting technology. Doubling every six weeks in 2012, running five databases at once, the team ripped out every clustered wonder mid-crisis and bet the company on the most boring stack available — manually sharded MySQL behind an ID that carries its own address. Then, on top of the boring, they built two quietly radical things: a home feed that is a scored pool instead of a timeline, and a recommender that answers by taking thousands of random walks across a three-billion-node graph held in RAM. This episode runs all three.

Everything below is honestly simulated: both feed worlds consume the identical pin stream and the engagement gap emerges from real top-k arithmetic, the Pixie panel runs genuine random walks over a seeded pin–board graph, and the hydration numbers are batched cache lookups against real hit rates.

Not a timeline — a pool with a scan head

In 2014 Pinterest tore up the chronological home feed. The replacement separates what you haven't seen from what you have: every new pin from people you follow is scored by the Pinnability models at write time and dropped into an HBase-backed pool, keyed (user, score, pin) — so the rows sort themselves best-first, and rendering your feed means reading the top of the table. Chronology stops mattering: a pin saved twenty minutes ago that scores higher than everything since will greet you at the top, not drown eight screens down. The experiment feeds both designs the same stream and lets the arithmetic speak.

Recommendations by random walk

Pinterest's catalog is a bipartite graph: pins live on boards, boards collect pins. Human curation already encoded taste into that structure — so Pixie (2017) recommends without any precomputation at all. Take the user's recent pins, release thousands of short random walks — pin to board to pin to board — with a bias to restart at the query pins so walks don't wander off into the void, and count where they land. High-visit pins are your recommendations: fresh, personal, computed in ~60ms against a 3-billion-node, 17-billion-edge graph pruned to fit in one machine's RAM. No joins — hydrate through the cache wall

The sharding scheme has a price written into it: there are no joins, no foreign keys, no cross-shard transactions. A board page — one board, fifty pins, fifty authors — can't be a JOIN, because those rows live on dozens of shards by design. Pinterest's answer is the pattern half the industry now runs: read the mapping table for IDs, then hydrate every object by ID through a thick memcached layer in one batched multi-get. The relational query becomes a hundred key lookups — which sounds worse until you remember key lookups are what caches, and 65,536 shards, are perfect at.

The great simplification

The context for all of it: in 2012, mid-hypergrowth, Pinterest was running MySQL, Cassandra, MongoDB, membase, and Redis at once — and the clustered systems kept failing in ways nobody could diagnose at 3 AM while traffic doubled every six weeks. The founding scaling decision wasn't a technology; it was a subtraction. They removed everything exotic and kept what was "mature, well-known, well-liked" — MySQL, memcached, Redis, Solr — because a system that fails in understood ways is worth more than one that's theoretically better. Everything in this episode stands on that call: the boring shards, the ID that routes itself, the cache wall. And on top of the boring, the adventurous: visual search that finds the lamp in your photo among billions of pins (one of the first production CNN retrieval systems, 2015; 600M+ visual searches a month by 2019), and Pixie's graph in RAM.

The keeper: be boring where you must be reliable, and radical only where it's the product — Pinterest sharded like an accountant and recommended like a gambler, and both choices were the same discipline.
