The Upload

YouTube is Netflix inverted. Netflix curates thousands of titles and ships them to your ISP overnight; YouTube must accept everything — more than 500 hours of new video arriving every minute — transcode all of it into a ladder of formats within minutes, store 20 billion videos forever, and serve a billion watch-hours a day. The firehose got so expensive that Google designed a custom chip just to keep up, and the database layer built to survive it escaped to become everyone's MySQL scaling tool.

Amber = video and the machines processing it. Cyan = viewers and requests. The transcoding, thumbnail, and playback experiments below are honest work simulations — chunks, seeks, and buffers behave by arithmetic, not by script.

Cut the tape: chunked parallel transcoding

A one-hour upload encoded on one machine at roughly real-time speed takes… an hour — and that's just the first rendition. YouTube's pipeline instead cuts the video into segments and encodes them simultaneously across the fleet, then stitches the results. Just as important is the order: a fast, cheap ladder is produced first so the video goes live in minutes, and the expensive VP9/AV1 renditions follow later — and only where predicted popularity justifies the compute.

The hard part isn't where you think: thumbnails

Serving the videos was comparatively easy — big sequential files, kind to disks. What nearly killed early YouTube was the small stuff: billions of ~5 KB thumbnails, dozens per page, each one a random disk seek. Apache buckled; the Squid cache in front of it started at 300 requests per second and degraded to 20 as the working set outgrew memory and every request became a seek. The post-acquisition fix: store thumbnails in BigTable, which packs millions of tiny images into large sorted files — so one disk read fetches a whole neighborhood of thumbnails, and caches span datacenters.

The last mile: the player does the adapting

After the pipeline built its ladder of renditions — 144p to 4K, H.264 and VP9 — the final system runs on the viewer's device. The player fetches video in small segments and, before each one, measures its bandwidth and picks a rung of the ladder. A fixed-quality player on a wobbling connection drains its buffer and freezes; the adaptive player trades pixels for continuity, stepping down before the buffer dies and back up when the network recovers. Same connection, same ladder — the difference is one decision every few seconds.

What held the metadata together

Under all of it sits the least glamorous, most exported piece: Vitess. Born at YouTube in 2010 when MySQL outages were multiplying, it wedged a Go proxy between application and database — pooling memory-expensive connections, rewriting and killing runaway queries, and hiding sharding and resharding from the application entirely. It carried all of YouTube's database traffic from 2011, was open-sourced, graduated the CNCF in 2019, and now runs MySQL fleets at Slack, GitHub, and beyond — one company's scar tissue becoming everyone's infrastructure (we met it holding Slack together back in the episodes' research base).

And the culture that scaled 2005-era YouTube to 4 billion views a day on a million lines of Python is worth its own sentence: pick the simplest thing with the loosest guarantees you can get away with; add jitter so the herd doesn't stampede; let view counters be briefly, invisibly wrong. Approximate correctness where nobody can observe it — exactness only where they can. That doctrine, plus a chip, plus a proxy, is how you swallow the firehose.
