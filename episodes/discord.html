The Guild

On Discord, a community is a "server" — and inside the machine, every one of them is literally a single Elixir process: one mailroom that routes every message, every typing flicker, every presence change for that community. It's a beautiful idea with a quadratic time bomb inside, because Midjourney's room grew to a million people online at once. This episode runs the mailroom until it jams, un-jams it the way Discord did, then runs the two other fires: the hot channel that melts a database partition, and the garbage collector that shook a latency graph every two minutes on the clock.

Everything below is honestly simulated: the mailroom is a real single-server queue (its service time really is N sends × 40µs), coalescing really collapses concurrent identical queries into one, and the GC seismograph is a queue with periodic server vacations — the spikes are arithmetic, not animation.

One mailroom per community

Discord's real-time core maps Erlang/OTP's actor model straight onto its social structure: every connected client is a session process, and every guild is a single GenServer that serializes and routes all events for that community. It's an elegant fit — no locks, no shared state, crash isolation per community. The catch surfaced on the way to five million concurrent users in 2017: a single send/2 to another process costs roughly 30–70µs, and the guild does one per online member, sequentially. For the biggest guild of the era — /r/Overwatch, around 30,000 concurrent — one event could cost up to ~2.1 seconds of the mailroom's time. Meanwhile more members also means more messages. Quadratic, meet wall.

Discord's fix, Manifold, changes who does the walking: instead of N sends to N sessions, the guild sends one bundle per server node, and each node unpacks it to its local sessions in parallel. The mailroom's per-event cost drops from N × 40µs to (number of nodes) × 40µs — the quadratic term moves out of the single process and gets divided across the cluster.

The ladder beneath: a billion messages, then a trillion

Below the mailrooms sits the other half of the promise: every message ever sent, retrievable instantly. Discord climbed the database ladder in public. MongoDB died first — at ~100 million messages (November 2015) the data and indexes outgrew RAM and latency went "unpredictable." Cassandra took over for six years, partitioned by ((channel_id, time-bucket)) — and taught two hard lessons. Deleted messages aren't gone, they're tombstones: one channel that had millions of deletions could stall a node just by being read. And partitions are only as balanced as your users: a dead-quiet friend server and a firehose community are the same schema but wildly different physics. By early 2022 the messages cluster was 177 nodes of Java GC firefighting.

The shield Discord built in front of storage is the subject of the next experiment: a Rust data-services tier that does request coalescing — when a raid, an @everyone, or a viral moment makes thousands of clients open the same channel at the same instant, the first query goes to the database and everyone else subscribes to that one answer. The same channel always routes to the same service instance (consistent hashing — episode 2's ring, again), so the hotness lands where the coalescing table is.

Two minutes on the clock

One more fire, this time in a service you'd never suspect: Read States — the thing that remembers, for every user in every channel, what you've read. It's touched on every connect, every send, every acknowledgement. The Go version held a huge LRU cache, and Go forces a garbage-collection cycle at least every two minutes — so every two minutes, on the clock, the service stopped to scan the entire cache and the latency graph grew a spike. Not load. Not traffic. The clock.

The rewrite is why "Discord switches from Go to Rust" became the most-cited systems blog of 2020: Rust has no collector — memory frees the moment an evicted entry's owner drops it — so the same workload runs flat. The spikes didn't shrink; they ceased to exist, and the team then grew the cache to 8 million entries because there was no longer a GC bill for holding it.

The nine-day migration

The ladder's last rung: in 2022 Discord moved trillions of messages from Cassandra to ScyllaDB — same data model, but C++, shard-per-core, and no garbage collector (EXP-03's lesson, applied to the database itself). They dual-wrote to both clusters, then a custom Rust migrator streamed history at up to 3.2 million messages per second — nine days for what the standard tooling estimated at three months — and cut over in May 2022 with zero downtime. The messages cluster shrank from 177 nodes to 72, p99 reads fell from 40–125ms to 15ms, p99 writes from 5–70ms to 5ms. The parts this episode didn't run are stories of their own: a custom C++ WebRTC SFU fleet was carrying 2.6 million concurrent voice users across 13 regions back in 2018 — clients never peer directly, which is both the scaling trick and the privacy guarantee.

The keeper: Discord's architecture is the actor model taken literally — a community is a process — and every scaling crisis since has been about protecting one hot point (a mailroom, a partition, a cache) from the crowd that loves it.
