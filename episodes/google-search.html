World of Internet Systems · The Flagships

The Index

Your question is answered in about 200 milliseconds against hundreds of billions of pages — more than five trillion times a year — because Google never searches the web. It searches a pre-built, sharded, in-memory copy of the web, assembled offline by crawlers and pipelines so that the online path does nothing but fast lookups and merges. This episode builds the actual data structures and runs the actual algorithms — a real inverted index, a real random surfer, real tail statistics.

Everything below computes for real: the index experiment builds posting lists and counts true operations, the PageRank surfer is a genuine random walk checked against power iteration, and the tail-latency samples are honest draws. The ranking function Google runs today is a trade secret; everything shown here is the published architecture.

The inversion

The oldest and best idea in search: don't scan documents at query time. Offline, flip the web inside out — from page → words into word → sorted list of pages ("posting lists"). A two-word query stops being "read everything" and becomes "walk two sorted lists and keep the pages they share." Brin and Page's 1998 prototype did exactly this over 24 million pages; the structure hasn't changed, only the scale.

The experiment below actually builds both worlds over a synthetic corpus: a grep machine that scans every word of every document, and a real inverted index with a real sorted-list intersection. Same queries, same answers — count the work.

The vote of the web

In 1997, ranking was the problem. Engines ranked by keyword statistics, so a page repeating your query fifty times beat the genuine authority — and spammers knew it. PageRank's move: ignore what a page says about itself and ask the web's own link structure. Imagine a surfer clicking random links forever, occasionally getting bored (15% of the time) and teleporting to a random page. The fraction of time the surfer spends on each page is its rank. Links became votes, votes flowed through the graph, and a page nobody linked to could shout keywords into the void.

The tail at scale

Fan-out has a cruel statistic. Suppose each shard answers fast except for a 1-in-100 hiccup — a garbage-collection pause, a background task. One shard is fine. But a query that must wait for all 100 shards gets hit whenever any of them hiccups: 1 − 0.99¹⁰⁰ ≈ 63% of queries. The rare event becomes the common case, and the slowest machine sets every query's latency. Dean and Barroso's published cure is disarmingly simple: hedge — if a shard hasn't answered within the expected time, send the same request to a replica and take whichever answers first.

The pipeline behind the curtain

The index doesn't exist until something builds it. Googlebot crawls from a prioritized URL frontier (politeness limits, robots.txt, revisit schedules — and since 2019 it renders pages in a real headless Chromium, so JavaScript content is indexed too). The papers that processed the result built the modern data industry: GFS (2003) stored it on cheap machines that fail constantly; MapReduce (2004) turned indexing into batch dataflow — over 20 petabytes a day by 2008; Bigtable (2006) held it; and Caffeine (2010) replaced batch index editions with continuous incremental updates, making results 50% fresher in a ~100-million-gigabyte live index. Their open-source echoes — Hadoop, HDFS, HBase — are that lineage wearing other logos.

And ranking kept moving: Knowledge Graph (2012, "things not strings"), RankBrain (2015), BERT on one in ten English queries (2019) — needed partly because 15% of each day's queries have never been seen before. The exact modern ranking function is secret; the machine that serves it is the one you just ran. The keeper pattern, purest form: precompute everything offline, so online is only lookups and merges.
