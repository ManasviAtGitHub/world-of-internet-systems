World of Internet Systems · The Wider World · Episode 28

The Graph

LinkedIn's feed problem was so hard that a side effect of solving it — Kafka, and the idea that one append-only log should be a company's circulatory system — became more famous than the feed. This episode runs the feed itself: the machine that answers "what did the 800 people and companies you follow just do?" in 140 milliseconds, by doing the exact opposite of what Twitter chose in episode 3 — fan-out on read, with the ranking pushed all the way down into the storage nodes, and yesterday's heaviest computations shipped in by atomic pointer flip.

Everything below is honestly simulated: scatter-gather latency is a genuine max-of-twelve with jitter, write amplification is counted follower by follower, the broker's network card saturates by arithmetic, and the in-place deploy really does serve mixed-version reads until the atomic swap removes the window entirely.

When every celebrity is an employer

Episode 3 watched Twitter choose push: write each tweet into every follower's timeline inbox, because reads outnumber writes 60:1 and celebrities can get an escape hatch. LinkedIn chose the opposite — and the reason is who its "celebrities" are. On a professional network the mega-followed accounts aren't a handful of pop stars; they're every major employer, and company updates are a huge share of all posts. Push that world and write amplification isn't an edge case — it's the workload. FollowFeed indexes each activity once, by its author, and assembles your feed at read time. Below, both designs face the same posts; then Microsoft shares a jobs update.

Send the ranking to the data

Pull has a hidden tax that killed naive implementations: moving the candidates. If each of twelve partitions ships its raw activities to the broker — hundreds of 2KB blobs each — every feed open moves ~10MB across the network before ranking can even start, and the broker's NIC becomes the whole system's ceiling. FollowFeed's fix is the oldest big-data commandment: move the computation, not the data. Each storage node runs relevance scoring locally, against its own slice, and returns only its top candidates — IDs and scores, a few kilobytes. The broker merges winners, not warehouses.

Yesterday's computation, shipped by pointer flip

Not everything can be computed at read time. "People You May Know" is a walk over the whole member graph — hundreds of milliseconds per member if done live. LinkedIn's Voldemort answered with a pattern that outlived the store itself: compute the entire feature for everyone offline in Hadoop, build the store's index and data files as part of the batch job, ship them to the serving nodes alongside the live version, warm them — and then flip a pointer. One atomic swap: between two requests, every member's recommendations change version, and no request ever sees half a build. The alternative — updating rows in place under live traffic — serves a window of mixed old-and-new that no amount of care closes.

The log that ate the company

And beneath all of it, the famous part. By 2010 LinkedIn's systems — warehouse, search, monitoring, recommenders — each grew bespoke pipelines to every other, N² integrations all breaking differently. Kafka reframed the problem: make every data change an event on one replicated, partitioned commit log, and let every system be a subscriber with its own bookmark. Episode 4 ran that machine's physics; this is the company that built it because the feed demanded it — and then wrote the manifesto ("The Log," 2013) that turned it into an industry. The numbers grew up accordingly: 7 trillion messages a day through LinkedIn's Kafka by 2019, feeding FollowFeed's indexers, Espresso's change streams, Samza, Hadoop, and everything this episode simulated. One monolith named Leo became 750+ services, and the log is what they all agree on.

The keeper: LinkedIn's feed teaches the pull side of the eternal fan-out argument — index once by author, scatter reads, score at the leaves — and its deeper lesson is that the hardest integration problem is solved by a data structure: one ordered log, many bookmarks.
