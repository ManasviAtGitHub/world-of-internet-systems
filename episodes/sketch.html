The Sketch

Find what's hot in a stream too big to remember. The count-min sketch keeps a few thousand counters for a universe of billions of keys — every event bumps d counters, every query reads their minimum — and it makes a one-sided promise no exact system at this size can: it never undercounts, ever. Collisions can only inflate. That asymmetry (the mirror image of the Filter's) is why it guards caches against hot keys, powers trending lists, and rate-limits firehoses. Predict first, then run it. That's the rep.

Everything below computes for real: real hash rows, an exact shadow count running alongside every sketch, and the never-undercounts guarantee is re-checked against the shadow on live samples — the violation counter is the proof it's mathematics, not luck.

Pattern
d hash rows · increment them all · read the minimum
Space
d × w counters — a few KB regardless of how many distinct keys exist
Time
O(d) per add and per query · merge = add the grids cell-wise
The catch
collisions only inflate — cold keys can look warmer than they are (≤ εn with ε = e/w), never colder

The price of a few kilobytes

The guarantee has a published price: with w columns, the overestimate on any key is at most εn where ε = e/w and n is the whole stream's length — with high probability (1 − e⁻ᵈ). But the bound is a worst case; the typical noise is what an engineer budgets for. So price it: one million events flow through a sketch of 2,000 × 5 counters (40 KB). A key that appeared exactly 100 times — what does the sketch report? Never less than 100; that much is theorem. Commit to how much more, then twelve independent trials measure it.

The raid alarm

The Guild episode watched a raid hammer one channel until request coalescing saved the cluster — but coalescing only helps once you know which key is hot. At a million requests a second you can't keep a counter per key to find out; the counter table would be the new hot spot. A count-min sketch with decay answers "is any key above X% of recent traffic?" in constant memory, and because it never undercounts, it can't miss a real hot key — the only possible failure is a false alarm. For an alarm, that's exactly the side you want to fail on.

Trending needs to forget

"Trending now" is a heavy-hitters query with a clock. A raw sketch never forgets — yesterday's mega-topic sits on its counters forever, and this morning's surge can't outrank it. The standard cure is beautifully blunt: halve every counter every few seconds. Recent events keep their weight; history decays geometrically; the sketch becomes a sliding spotlight instead of an archive. The Front Page episode's rankings fight exactly this fight with their time-decay scoring — same law, different structure. The keeper

The keeper: the count-min sketch answers "how often?" for any key in a boundless stream using d rows of w counters — never undercounting, overcounting by at most εn, finding heavy hitters an exact table couldn't afford to look for, and learning to forget with a periodic halving. It completes the sketch family: the Filter answers "have we seen it?", the Counter answers "how many distinct?", and the Sketch answers "how often?" — all three in kilobytes, all three one-sided about how they're allowed to be wrong.

The revision card — for the you that comes back in three months
  • Why can it never undercount? (counters only increment; the min of inflated cells is still ≥ the truth — you watched 0 violations)
  • What's the error law, and what does w buy? (overestimate ≤ εn, ε = e/w — the planted 100 read a few hundred, under a 1,459 ceiling)
  • Why is min-over-rows the whole trick? (each row carries ~n/w of noise, but a key is only hurt by its quietest cell)
  • How does "trending" forget? (halve everything every few seconds — geometric decay is the clock)
