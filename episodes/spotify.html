The Playlist

In 2008 a Swedish startup promised music that starts faster than your hard drive — on infrastructure it couldn't possibly afford. The trick: make the listeners the CDN. Spotify ran the biggest legal P2P network in the world for six years, then deleted it without sentimentality, deleted its datacenters too, and rebuilt itself as a data-pipeline company whose flagship feature is a Monday-morning batch job. This episode runs all three acts: the 265-millisecond illusion, the event river that must never clog, and a Discover Weekly you can actually watch being computed.

Everything below is honestly simulated: the latency split really is server-start-plus-background-search, the byte accounting reproduces the published ~9% figure from its own arithmetic, the pipeline backlogs are real queues, and EXP-03 runs a genuine nearest-neighbor recommendation over an embedded catalog — the picks are computed, not decorated.

A CDN made of listeners

The hard part of P2P isn't moving bytes — it's finding them. When you click play, asking a tracker and gossiping with neighbors for peers takes most of a second, and a music player that hesitates for a second feels broken. Spotify's founding trick was to refuse the choice between fast and cheap: the client fetches the first ~15 seconds from Spotify's servers immediately — click→sound is server latency, ~150–250ms — while the P2P search runs behind the music. By the time the server chunk runs out, peers are found and the swarm carries the rest of the track. Latency comes from the fast source; bytes come from the cheap one.

Delete the network, delete the datacenters

Then Spotify killed it. By 2014, bandwidth prices had collapsed, and listening had moved to phones — where P2P never ran. The desktop swarm that once carried ~80% of traffic was a shrinking island of complexity, so in April 2014 it was shut down: pure client-server + CDN from then on. The right architecture for one era, retired cleanly in the next — and then the same knife again, deeper: from 2016 to 2018 Spotify moved ~1,200 microservices and 20,000 data jobs out of its four datacenters into Google's cloud (a reported ~$450M commitment) and closed the last machine room. What was left to engineer, once serving bytes became a line item? The data. Half a trillion events a day of it.

The event river

Every tap in Spotify — play, skip, search, playlist-add — becomes an event, and one of those types is sacred: the royalty-bearing play, the record that pays the artists. The first event pipeline (2016-era: syslog into Kafka 0.7 into Hadoop) moved 700K events/sec through one shared channel — so any surge or sink failure anywhere delayed everything, including the sacred stream. The rebuilt system gives every event type its own Pub/Sub topic and its own pipeline: bulkheads. A busy stream can only sink itself. By 2019 it carried 8M events/sec at peak — 500 billion events, 350 terabytes, every day.

Thirty songs every Monday

The payoff for all that plumbing ships weekly. Discover Weekly (July 2015) is a batch job: once a week, a pipeline regenerates a 30-song playlist for every user — 40 million listeners and five billion streams in its first year. Three model families vote: collaborative filtering embeds songs near each other when the same people listen to them (mined from 2+ billion user-made playlists — taste statements, free); NLP embeds what the internet writes about music; and a convolutional net over the raw audio — which matters for one specific reason the experiment below makes visible: a song released on Friday has no listening history, so collaborative filtering literally cannot see it. The audio model can.

What the story teaches

One postscript from the org chart: a thousand squad-owned microservices made the map the scarce artifact, so Spotify built Backstage — a searchable catalog of everything — and open-sourced it in 2020. Conway's law, embraced on purpose and then given tooling. Today the machine serves ~700 million monthly listeners, and its signature feature still ships the way this episode ran it: as a batch job, once a week, quietly.

The keeper: Spotify never had one architecture — it had the right one three times: peers when bytes were expensive, cloud when engineers were, and pipelines when the product became the data. The skill isn't the design; it's the willingness to delete it.
