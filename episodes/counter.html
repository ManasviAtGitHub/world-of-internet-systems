The Counter

Count a million distinct visitors in twelve kilobytes, off by less than one percent — without remembering a single one of them. HyperLogLog is the structure behind every "unique viewers" number at scale (Redis ships it as PFCOUNT; Google's BigQuery and half the analytics industry run its descendants), and its trick is gloriously indirect: it never counts anything. It watches for record-breaking runs of zeros in hashes, because record holders know how big the crowd is. Predict first, then run it. That's the rep.

Everything below computes for real: real hashes, real registers, the published estimator formula with its small-range correction — and the true distinct count runs alongside every estimate, so the error you read is measured, never quoted.

The interview card — where you've ground this before
  • "Count distinct visitors across a billion events — you don't get a billion IDs of RAM."
  • "Why doesn't adding the same user twice break the count?" (It can't — watch the replay below.)
  • "Uniques per shard are counted — how many uniques across shards?" (Sums lie; there's a merge that doesn't.)
Pattern
hashing · leading-zero records · harmonic mean of m observers
Space
m registers × ~6 bits — 16,384 registers ≈ 12 KB for billions
Time
O(1) add · O(m) count · merge = register-wise max
The catch
it's an estimate (±1.04/√m), and it can't enumerate, delete, or answer "is X in the set" — that last one is The Filter's job

Why record holders know the crowd

Flip coins until you get heads. A run of 10 tails is a 1-in-1,024 event — so if the best run anyone in the room has seen is 10, there were probably about a thousand people flipping. Hashes make every distinct ID one such flipper (and the same ID always flips the same coins — that's the idempotence). One record-holder is a terrible witness — records are noisy — so HyperLogLog splits the crowd into m independent rooms and blends their records with a harmonic mean. The blend has a published accuracy: ±1.04/√m. So price the industry default: m = 16,384 registers — 12 KB, Redis's setting — counting one million distinct IDs. How far off is the answer, typically? Commit; then sixteen independent million-ID trials run for real and their errors land on the chart.

The merge that doesn't lie

Here's the production question that breaks naive designs: twelve servers each count their own uniques — how many uniques across all twelve? Summing the counters is wrong the moment one visitor touches two servers, and real visitors touch several. Exact answers mean shipping the actual ID sets across the network. HyperLogLog has an almost unfair answer: take the twelve register arrays and keep the maximum of each register. A record across all rooms is just the best record any room saw — so the merged sketch is exactly the sketch of the union, still 12 KB, no IDs shipped. This is Redis's PFMERGE, and it's why "uniques this week" and "uniques across the fleet" are cheap questions at the Front Page episode's scale.

Why m rooms, not one

The whole cleverness lives in the averaging, and you can watch it. A single record-holder estimates the crowd as 2^record — but records jump in whole powers of two and one lucky streak wrecks the reading. Sixteen rooms tame it a little. A thousand and twenty-four settle it. Below, three real estimators watch the same growing stream — and if the "tallest tower" phrasing sounds familiar, it should: the longest zero-run among n hashes is the same random variable as the tallest tower in The Skip List — about log₂ n. One structure balances with it; this one counts with it.

The keeper

The keeper: HyperLogLog counts distinct things by remembering only the best runs of leading zeros — O(1) adds that duplicates cannot move, ±1.04/√m accuracy in kilobytes, and a register-wise-max merge that computes the union without the data. When the interview says "count uniques at scale," this is the shape; it cannot list members, cannot delete, and cannot answer membership — the Filter answers that, the Counter answers "how many."

The revision card — for the you that comes back in three months
  • Why can a duplicate never move the estimate? (same ID → same hash → same register, same run — a rerun can't break its own record)
  • What does m buy, and at what rate? (error ≈ 1.04/√m — you watched one register swing in octaves and 16,384 land under 1%)
  • Why is the fleet-wide union cheap? (merge = register-wise max = exactly the union's sketch; SUM overcounted by the overlap factor)
  • Which questions can't it answer? (membership, enumeration, deletion — cardinality only; pair it with The Filter for "have we seen X?")
