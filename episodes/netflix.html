World of Internet Systems · The Flagships · Episode 06

Press Play

Netflix is two systems glued together at one button. A control plane of microservices on AWS decides what you watch — and a private CDN called Open Connect, made of free servers living inside your ISP, delivers every single video byte. One company, roughly 15% of the world's downstream internet traffic, and the reason it feels instant is that most nights the video was already in your neighborhood before you wanted it.

Amber = video bytes. Cyan = control traffic (auth, steering). The backbone and server capacities below are honest fluid models, scaled down so one stream ≈ many thousands of real ones.

Why build your own CDN out of free boxes?

In 2012 Netflix stopped paying commercial CDNs and started shipping its own purpose-built FreeBSD servers — Open Connect Appliances — to ISPs, for free. ISPs host them happily, because every stream served from inside their network is a stream they don't have to haul across expensive transit links. By 2016, about 90% of Netflix traffic flowed over direct Netflix-to-ISP connections, 100% of video went through Open Connect, and a single appliance had gone from 8 Gbps (2012) to 90+ Gbps of throughput.

The experiment below is the counterfactual: the same evening audience, with and without the boxes in the neighborhood. The backbone link is the honest bottleneck.

The CDN that fills itself at 3 AM

Classic CDNs are demand-filled: the first viewer of a title in each city pays a cache-miss — the CDN fetches it across the backbone at the worst possible moment, peak evening, precisely because that's when first viewers show up. Netflix inverted the model. Every night, during the ISP's quiet window, each appliance asks AWS what tomorrow's audience will probably watch — popularity prediction per region — and pre-positions those titles. The fill traffic rides the empty night; the evening peak becomes almost purely local reads.

And then they attack their own servers

The control plane earns its reliability differently: by being attacked, on purpose, during business hours. After a 2008 database corruption halted DVD shipping for three days, Netflix rebuilt on AWS with a rule the industry now calls chaos engineering — if instances will die randomly anyway, kill them yourself, constantly, so nobody can ship a service that can't survive it. Chaos Monkey (2010) kills instances; Chaos Gorilla kills availability zones; Chaos Kong evacuates entire AWS regions — a drill Netflix runs monthly and completes in about six minutes. Try being the monkey.

What the play button hides

One more number for scale: before any of this, every title is chunked and encoded on a ~300,000-CPU pipeline into dozens of codec/bitrate/resolution renditions — one season of Stranger Things became 9,570 files and took 190,000 CPU-hours. Since 2015 the bitrate ladder is computed per title (a cartoon needs a fraction of an action film's bits at equal quality), and since 2018 per shot, guided by their open-sourced perceptual metric VMAF — roughly 30% of bitrate saved at Netflix scale is a measurable share of the entire internet. And the player on your TV is doing the last trick alone: measuring bandwidth continuously, sliding up and down the ladder, and failing over between Open Connect servers mid-stream without telling you.

The pattern to keep from this episode: split the brain from the muscle. Three AWS regions decide; a thousand embedded boxes deliver; they meet exactly once, at Play. Every large media system since has converged on the same split.
