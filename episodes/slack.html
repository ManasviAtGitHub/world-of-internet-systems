The Workspace

Slack is proudly boring on the outside — a PHP monolith and MySQL, the stack of a 2004 blog. The interesting part is the nervous system grafted onto it: a message you type is durably written first, then fanned out across the planet in roughly half a second. This episode runs the tour, then the three crises the boring stack survived: the boot payload that grew into a truck, the reconnect storm that used to melt the origin, and the day the job queue wedged itself because the reservoir was also the turbine.

Everything below is honestly simulated: the world tour's clock adds up real hop latencies, boot times are computed from payload bytes and bandwidth, the storm is a genuine queue against fixed origin capacity, and the wedge is the real coupling — a queue whose dequeue needs the memory the backlog just ate.

The truck that delivered the whole office

Until 2017, connecting to Slack meant calling rtm.start — which returned the entire workspace: every user, every channel, every membership, up front, so the client could work offline-fast. For a 40-person startup that's a pamphlet. For the enterprises Slack was winning, it became a truck: payloads in the tens of megabytes, boot times in the tens of seconds, mobile clients timing out and retrying — which made it worse.

Flannel is Slack's answer, and it's not "make the payload smaller" — it's "stop answering from the origin." Flannel is an application-aware cache deployed at edge POPs, on the WebSocket path itself. Clients get a minimal boot payload and then lazily query Flannel for users, channels, and bots as the UI actually needs them; consistent hashing sends a team's members to the same warm cache, which keeps itself fresh by consuming the same event stream the clients do — and it even pushes objects just-in-time (the profile of someone who was just @mentioned arrives before you ask). By 2017 it held five million simultaneous connections and answered a million queries a second.

The storm after the blink

The truck had a second, nastier failure mode. Payloads download once per connect — and connects cluster. An office network blips, a regional ISP hiccups, Slack deploys — and thousands of clients reconnect in the same ten seconds, each demanding its truck. The origin has to serialize every one of those payloads; a reconnect storm turned into a self-inflicted DDoS, and recovering clients that timed out reconnected again. With Flannel at the edge, the same storm hits a wall of warm caches instead: boots are cheap, the origin barely registers, and the storm is over when the last client's stagger timer fires.

The reservoir that was also the turbine

Everything Slack defers — push notifications, link unfurls, search indexing, calendar reminders — goes through a job queue: 1.4 billion jobs a day, 33,000 per second at peak (2017). It ran on Redis, and it had a fatal coupling nobody noticed until the day it mattered: dequeuing a job required free Redis memory (a worker moves the job into a processing list before running it). So when a traffic spike filled Redis, workers could no longer take jobs out. Enqueues failing was expected; the backlog freezing solid was not. The dam was also the powerhouse, and the flood shut down both.

The fix is a model of incremental re-architecture: don't rewrite the workers — put Kafka in front. Kafkagate accepts enqueues onto a durable, disk-backed log (the Kafka episode's log, wearing a hard hat); JQRelay meters jobs into Redis only as fast as Redis has room; the worker fleet never learns anything changed. Spikes become lag. Lag drains.

The slow escape from the workspace shard

One story this episode leaves in prose, because the Ladder episode already ran its physics: Slack's founding data model pinned each workspace to one master-master MySQL pair — clean isolation, easy scaling, until one giant customer was one melting database, and shared channels between companies had no home at all. From 2017 to 2020 Slack moved ~99% of query load onto Vitess, re-sharding by finer keys like channel and user: 2.3 million queries per second at 2ms median by the end. The real-time tier made the same pivot earlier — message servers were sharded by team until shared channels forced the channel-centric design the hero panel runs.

The keeper: Slack's whole story is moving answers closer to the question — the truck became an edge cache, the workspace shard became a channel shard, and the one queue that couldn't buffer got a log in front of it. The boring stack survived because every crisis was solved around it, not through it.
