World of Internet Systems · Internet Plumbing · Episode 14

The Page

"What happens when you type a URL and press Enter?" is the most famous question in systems interviews because the honest answer touches everything this series has built: a keyboard bus, a global name database, three handshakes, BGP-chosen routes, a server farm, and a GPU — usually in under a second. This episode walks the whole path with a scrubber in your hand, then runs the three wars that made it fast: the war on round trips, the war on head-of-line blocking, and the war on the blocking script.

All times are honest arithmetic over the published budgets: ~10ms keyboard poll, 20–120ms uncached DNS, one RTT per handshake, a 10ms usable frame budget. Drag the scrubber — the journey is yours to replay.

The war on round trips

A page's speed is mostly counted in round trips, not megabits. Before the first byte of HTML arrives, the classic stack pays: one RTT for TCP's handshake, two for TLS 1.2's negotiation, one for the request itself — plus DNS. The last decade of protocol engineering is a war on those trips: TLS 1.3 (2018) sends its key shares speculatively and closes in one RTT; QUIC refuses to treat transport setup and crypto setup as separate conversations, fusing both into a single flight over UDP; and 0-RTT resumption rides the request on the very first packets. Distance is the multiplier — drag the slider to a satellite link and watch the old stack drown in its own handshakes.

The war on head-of-line blocking

A real page is not one fetch — it's dozens, racing over a lossy network. HTTP/1 opened six TCP connections and queued resources behind them. HTTP/2 multiplexed everything onto one connection — elegant, until a single lost packet made TCP halt every stream while it waited for the retransmission, because TCP promises in-order bytes and doesn't know the streams exist. HTTP/3's QUIC gives each stream its own delivery order: a lost packet stalls only the resource it belonged to. Same page, same losses, three protocols.

The war on the blocking script

The bytes have arrived; now the browser must not squander them. The parser builds the DOM incrementally as HTML streams in — but a synchronous <script> in the head stops it cold: the script might document.write, so the parser must fetch it, run it, and only then continue. One slow script tag used to freeze entire pages — the reason "put scripts at the bottom" became folk wisdom, later codified as defer. The modern browser also cheats on your behalf: a preload scanner races ahead of the blocked parser, starting fetches early either way.

The tower of translations

Step back and the whole journey is one idea repeated: translation between layers that only trust the contract below them. Names to addresses (DNS), addresses to next hops (BGP, ARP), packets to reliable streams (TCP, QUIC), streams to secrecy (TLS), secrecy to semantics (HTTP), markup to pixels (the engine). That modularity is why each layer could evolve independently for forty years — TLS could halve its handshakes, QUIC could replace TCP, browsers could grow preload scanners — without anyone rewriting the internet. And it's why, when one early layer fails, everything above it appears to die at once: the day Facebook's routes vanished, the world's browsers reported a hundred different errors that were all the same missing answer at stage two.

This closes the plumbing tour that began with a load balancer fourteen episodes ago. The keeper for the whole tier: the internet feels instant because every layer streams into the next and nobody waits for completeness — DOM before the last byte, paint before the last image, answers before certainty.
