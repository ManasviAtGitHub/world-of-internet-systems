The Meeting

Zoom's servers never look at your video. That single refusal — no decoding, no mixing, no re-encoding, just routing encrypted packets — is why a 500-person meeting became a checkbox instead of a hardware purchase, and why, when the world moved into Zoom in March 2020 and load multiplied thirty-fold in three months, the fix was simply more boxes that forward bytes. This episode runs the refusal: the MCU that melts vs the router that doesn't, the simulcast trick that keeps a 100-person gallery inside a home connection, and the 30× cliff itself.

Everything below is honestly simulated: server load is computed from per-stream decode/encode/forward costs, the gallery's bandwidth demand is real arithmetic against your modeled connection, and the cliff replays the published 10M→300M curve against capacity that must be provisioned day by day.

The server that refuses to watch

Conferencing before Zoom ran on MCUs — Multipoint Control Units — servers that decode every participant's video, composite one picture, and re-encode it for every receiver. All the work of a TV studio, per meeting, per box: quality-lossy, compute-monstrous, and capped somewhere under 100 participants. Zoom's founding bet (Eric Yuan, ex-WebEx, 2011) was the Multimedia Router: a distributed SFU that takes one encoded uplink per client and selectively forwards it — the speaker's high-resolution stream to everyone, thumbnails for the gallery — never opening a single frame. Encoding and decoding happen on your laptop. The server's cost scales with bandwidth, not pixels; Zoom claimed ~15× the participants per box, and the experiment below shows exactly where that number lives.

One uplink, tailored downlinks

The router's refusal creates a puzzle at your end of the wire. If the server won't composite, and 25 people are in the gallery, must your home connection receive 25 full-resolution streams? The answer is simulcast: every client encodes its camera at several sizes at once — full, medium, thumbnail — and the router picks, per receiver, which layer of which sender to forward: the active speaker in full, two dozen thumbnails, nothing at all for camera 26. Your downlink stops depending on how big the meeting is. The sender pays honestly for this — three encodings cost ~30% more uplink than one — and it's the best bandwidth ever spent.

The 30× cliff

Then came the quarter when the architecture was audited by history. December 2019: ~10 million daily meeting participants, served from ~17 colocated datacenters deliberately run at 50% of peak so any site can absorb a neighbor's failure. March 2020: 200 million. April: over 300 million. No capacity plan survives 30× in three months — what saved Zoom is that its scarce resource was generic. Because MMRs only forward bytes, "more capacity" meant ordinary cloud servers, and Zoom bought them like electricity: AWS turning up thousands of servers a day, an emergency Oracle deal carrying ~7 petabytes a day within weeks. What the meeting taught the internet

Two footnotes belong in prose. First, the trust lesson: in April 2020, Citizen Lab found some meetings routed through datacenters in China and weaknesses in the then-AES-128-ECB encryption. Zoom's response made routing topology a product feature — paying customers can now pin or exclude regions — and Zoom 5.0 shipped AES-256-GCM within a month, with end-to-end encryption that October. Where your packets go became something you configure, not something you trust. Second, the firewall ladder that explains Zoom's "it just works" reputation: UDP first; blocked → TCP; blocked → TLS on port 443, indistinguishable from ordinary web traffic. The client negotiates its way out of almost any network, and the server stays dumb.

The keeper: Zoom scaled because its servers do as little as possible — a box that refuses to understand video can be replaced by any other box on earth, and in March 2020 it had to be.
